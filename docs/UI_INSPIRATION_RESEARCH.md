# UI Inspiration Research

Research date: 2026-09-21

## Purpose and scope

This is a focused visual and interaction review for **My First Investment App**, a family financial-learning simulator rather than a real-money product. The reference set contains exactly:

- 10 consumer finance apps that balance color or personality with professional presentation.
- 5 children's apps that make complex learning or open-ended play approachable.

The notes below are based only on official product pages, official help centers, and first-party product announcements. Descriptions of visual treatment are observations of the product imagery on those pages, not claims about every screen in each app.

## Ten colorful, professional finance references

### 1. Monzo

- **Official sources:** [Monzo Pots](https://monzo.com/features/pots), [Monzo home](https://monzo.com/index.html)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Pots turn abstract budget categories into named containers. Users can add their own image to a Pot, so a goal becomes something recognizable rather than another row of numbers. The product imagery pairs a clean banking feed with clear balance graphs and card-like containers.
- **Color use:** Monzo's hot-coral identity supplies a memorable focal color while the app screens keep most working surfaces comparatively quiet. Personal photos provide additional color without forcing the interface itself to use many competing accents.
- **Interaction tone:** Conversational and lightly witty; examples such as a holiday, date night, groceries, or even a dog's fund make budgeting feel connected to life.
- **Transferable lesson:** Let a child name a goal and optionally choose an icon or image. Personal meaning can provide more delight than decorating every control.

### 2. Wealthsimple

- **Official sources:** [Wealthsimple app redesign](https://product-news.wealthsimple.com/meet-our-re-designed-wealthsimple-app-appui), [Wealthsimple home](https://www.wealthsimple.com/en-ca)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** The redesigned app separates **Home**, **Spend**, and **Invest** while keeping a total-wealth overview. The official product imagery shows a restrained shell around clear totals, account groupings, charts, and portfolio illustrations.
- **Color use:** Neutral backgrounds and strong typography carry the core experience; color is concentrated in charts, account art, and editorial illustrations rather than spread across every surface.
- **Interaction tone:** Calm, editorial, and confident. The hierarchy says “understand the big picture first, then drill in.”
- **Transferable lesson:** A playful product can still have a quiet structural layer. Keep navigation and totals stable, then spend color on learning moments and meaningful state.

### 3. Cash App

- **Official sources:** [Cash App home](https://cash.app/), [How Cash App works](https://cash.app/learn/getting-started/what-is-cash-app-and-how-does-it-work)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Large, direct actions sit beside customizable cards, stickers, payment text, and a short path to sending or requesting. Its official pages emphasize personalization without hiding the primary action.
- **Color use:** A high-energy green, black, and white system creates immediate recognition; card designs, stickers, and imagery introduce controlled variety.
- **Interaction tone:** Fast, casual, and expressive. The product explicitly supports personalized cards and payments with stickers and text.
- **Transferable lesson:** Reserve one vivid brand color for action and identity. Give kids safe personalization around goals or avatars, while keeping money-changing actions unambiguous.

### 4. Acorns

- **Official sources:** [Acorns home](https://www.acorns.com/), [Acorns Round-Ups](https://www.acorns.com/round-ups/), [Acorns Invest](https://www.acorns.com/invest/)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** The product explains an automated behavior as a three-step story: link, collect small amounts, then grow over time. The oak-growth metaphor ties together education, progress, and long-term outcomes.
- **Color use:** A botanical, green-forward identity is paired with brighter supporting accents and illustrated product moments. Color reinforces the “small beginnings become growth” narrative.
- **Interaction tone:** Reassuring and incremental; the language repeatedly frames progress as easy, automatic small steps.
- **Transferable lesson:** Interest and compounding are easier to grasp as a visible growth journey. Show “today,” “over time,” and “toward my goal” rather than only presenting a balance.

### 5. Robinhood

- **Official sources:** [Robinhood charts](https://robinhood.com/us/en/support/articles/using-charts/), [Robinhood's milestone redesign](https://robinhood.com/us/en/newsroom/a-new-way-to-celebrate-with-robinhood/), [first-party product/design filing](https://investors.robinhood.com/static-files/371e7a9b-2fc9-48cd-a0af-077a7e172f6e)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Mobile-first screens put the current value and a simple line chart above deeper statistics and actions. Robinhood also created specific visual treatments for important first-time milestones instead of using one generic celebration everywhere.
- **Color use:** Bright green is used as a strong brand and positive-performance signal against a sparse, high-contrast interface.
- **Interaction tone:** Sleek, immediate, and milestone-oriented.
- **Transferable lesson:** A simple chart can make change over time legible, but celebration must be proportional to the learning event. Celebrate completing a lesson or reaching a savings plan—not merely moving simulated money or taking risk.

### 6. YNAB

- **Official sources:** [YNAB features](https://www.ynab.com/features), [YNAB goal tracking](https://www.ynab.com/features/goal-tracking), [YNAB mobile remodel](https://www.ynab.com/whats-new/the-great-ynab-remodel)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Categories, pinned priorities, alerts, a current goal, and monthly summaries turn a dense budget into a prioritized home screen. Goal screens use color-coded progress bars and calculate what is needed over time.
- **Color use:** Multiple hues communicate category identity and progress, while labels and amounts continue to carry the actual meaning. Official materials describe reports and charts as “full technicolor.”
- **Interaction tone:** Encouraging and forgiving. The goal experience explicitly acknowledges that plans change and lets users snooze a target without guilt.
- **Transferable lesson:** Teach adjustment, not perfection. A child should be able to try a plan, see the result, and revise it without the interface framing the experiment as failure.

### 7. Copilot Money

- **Official sources:** [Copilot Money home](https://www.copilot.money/), [Copilot quick-start guide](https://help.copilot.money/en/articles/11157550-quick-start-guide), [Copilot FAQ](https://www.copilot.money/faq)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** A unified dashboard combines transactions, budgets, investments, goals, and cash flow. Transaction rows pair recognizable emoji with category names and amounts; a review queue gives the user one clear next job.
- **Color use:** Product imagery uses dark, calm surfaces with bright chart, category, and emoji accents. Color is attached to recognizable objects or data rather than used as general decoration.
- **Interaction tone:** Polished, calm, and attentive. Automation reduces busywork, but review remains a deliberate user action.
- **Transferable lesson:** Emoji or simple icons are excellent category shorthand when backed by plain labels. A small “needs your attention” queue would also work well for parent approvals.

### 8. Qapital

- **Official sources:** [Qapital saving](https://www.qapital.com/saving/), [Qapital Rules and Goals](https://help.qapital.com/en/collections/11080668-rules-goals), [Qapital saving rules](https://www.qapital.com/blog/save-money-qapital-rules/)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Personalized goals are paired with progress and with understandable “if this, then save” rules. The system offers several paths—scheduled saving, round-ups, spending less, or behavior-linked rules—under a common goal model.
- **Color use:** Goal imagery and friendly illustrations provide emotional color, while repeated goal/rule cards provide consistency.
- **Interaction tone:** Aspirational and inventive. Saving is framed as a customizable game plan rather than deprivation.
- **Transferable lesson:** The Pretend Zone could grow into small, named experiments: “If I save $5 each week…” or “If I skip one purchase…”. Keep the result hypothetical and explain the rule in one sentence.

### 9. Revolut

- **Official sources:** [Revolut US](https://www.revolut.com/en-US/), [Revolut spending analytics](https://help.revolut.com/en-US/help/accounts/budget-and-analytics/how-can-i-see-my-spending-and-income-analytics/), [Revolut 2025 annual report](https://assets.revolut.com/pdf/annualreport2025.pdf)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** The product places modular money tools—cards, savings, analytics, and investments—inside one consistent mobile shell. Analytics allow bar, pie, and line views and can be organized by category, merchant, country, currency, or card.
- **Color use:** The neutral shell leaves room for vivid card artwork, goal imagery, and neon or gradient campaign accents. Color often identifies the object being managed rather than recoloring the whole screen.
- **Interaction tone:** Energetic, global, and lifestyle-oriented, while security sections deliberately become more sober.
- **Transferable lesson:** Match emotional tone to context. Exploration can be colorful; password, guardian approval, and destructive actions should remain visually calm and explicit.

### 10. Plum

- **Official sources:** [What is Plum?](https://help.withplum.com/en/articles/8286737-what-is-plum), [Plum Pockets](https://help.withplum.com/en/articles/8707730-what-is-a-pocket), [Plum home](https://withplum.com/)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Dedicated Pockets make goals concrete, while automation is personified as the “Plum Brain.” The official experience organizes saving into approachable rules and destinations rather than exposing financial machinery first.
- **Color use:** A purple/plum brand family, rounded illustration, and playful emoji soften an otherwise structured account-and-goal product.
- **Interaction tone:** Friendly, lightly whimsical, and automation-led.
- **Transferable lesson:** A memorable name can make a learning tool approachable. “Pretend Zone” already has that advantage; its content should look like a safe simulator, not another account form.

## Five fun children's-app references

### 1. Khan Academy Kids

- **Official source:** [Khan Academy Kids](https://learn.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-kids/)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Five recurring characters guide children through an adaptive path, while a separate library supports self-directed browsing across activities, books, videos, and coloring pages.
- **Color use:** Friendly characters and illustrated activity tiles carry a broad, cheerful palette; the repeated cast provides cohesion.
- **Interaction tone:** Warm, guided, and encouraging without talking down to the child.
- **Transferable lesson:** A small recurring guide can explain interest, saving, or purchase requests. One guide is more coherent than adding unrelated decorative icons everywhere.

### 2. Sago Mini World

- **Official sources:** [Sago Mini World](https://sagomini.com/world/), [Sago Mini World parent guide](https://sagomini.com/article/world-letter-to-parents/)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Children swipe through worlds, use a quick-access tray, and explore open-ended scenes. The app is designed so young children can navigate independently, with parent participation adding conversational depth.
- **Color use:** Saturated character art and scene-specific palettes create variety; consistent illustration style and large characters keep the collection unified.
- **Interaction tone:** Silly, curious, and pressure-free. The product explicitly avoids rules, time limits, and points.
- **Transferable lesson:** Fun does not require points or streak pressure. Let children explore “what if?” scenarios at their own pace, with parents able to discuss the result.

### 3. Pok Pok

- **Official sources:** [Pok Pok](https://playpokpok.com/), [Pok Pok press kit](https://playpokpok.com/press/press-kit/)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** The playroom uses handcrafted digital toys with no instructions, menus, or language in the child experience. Interactions are based on recognizable cause and effect, with no wrong answer or failure state.
- **Color use:** Hand-drawn art, softer colors, and gentle sound intentionally create a calm experience rather than an overstimulating reward loop.
- **Interaction tone:** Quiet, exploratory, and trusting of the child's curiosity.
- **Transferable lesson:** The app does not need to look like a candy store to feel child-friendly. Tactile controls, immediate cause-and-effect, and calm illustration can make financial experimentation inviting.

### 4. Duolingo ABC

- **Official sources:** [Duolingo ABC](https://abc.duolingo.com/h), [How Duolingo ABC teaches](https://abc.duolingo.com/how-we-teach)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Bite-sized activities, character-driven stories, and scaffolded levels turn a difficult curriculum into small actions children can complete independently.
- **Color use:** A recognizable green brand is supported by bright character illustrations and activity-specific colors, while each lesson retains a singular focus.
- **Interaction tone:** Humorous, upbeat, and game-like, but grounded in a clear learning sequence.
- **Transferable lesson:** Break financial concepts into tiny loops: choose an amount, predict, see the result, and receive a one-sentence explanation.

### 5. Toca Boca World

- **Official sources:** [Toca Boca World](https://www.tocaboca.com/toca-boca-world), [Toca Boca apps](https://www.tocaboca.com/apps)
- **Visual/UI patterns:** Locations, characters, objects, and creation tools let children build their own stories instead of following one prescribed win path. Distinct scenes contain the visual abundance.
- **Color use:** A wide, vibrant palette works because each location is its own coherent scene and because bold character art remains the anchor.
- **Interaction tone:** Expressive, quirky, inclusive, and creator-led.
- **Transferable lesson:** Let children make the app theirs through goal names, icons, and “why I want this” stories. Do not copy the visual density; borrow the sense of ownership.

## Shared patterns worth carrying forward

1. **Color follows meaning.** The strongest finance examples attach color to a goal, category, chart, card, or state. They do not simply make neighboring inputs different colors.
2. **Stable structure, playful content.** Wealthsimple and Copilot keep navigation and information hierarchy quiet; Monzo, Cash App, and the children's apps put personality inside goals, imagery, icons, and moments.
3. **Progress beats raw totals.** Acorns, YNAB, Monzo, and Qapital make a destination or journey visible. For a learner, “$20 of $50 for a new toy” is more motivating and educational than “balance: $20.”
4. **Children need agency, not casino mechanics.** Sago Mini, Pok Pok, and Toca Boca show that exploration and ownership can be compelling without streak pressure or random rewards.
5. **A family product needs two densities, not two brands.** The child side can use larger controls, illustrations, and guided explanations. The parent side should use the same semantic colors and components with denser queues, history, and approvals.

## Recommendation

Start with **Growing Garden**, borrowing the structural restraint of Bright Ledger.

The current application already uses blue for saving and warm amber/orange for spending. Growing Garden preserves that learned meaning and adds an evergreen brand color for primary navigation and growth. That creates a useful four-part system without inventing a confusing “tertiary versus quaternary” hierarchy:

- **Evergreen:** product identity, primary actions, overall growth.
- **Blue:** saving and money kept for later.
- **Orange:** spending and purchase impact.
- **Gold:** interest, milestones, and teaching callouts.

Neutral surfaces should do most of the layout work. Color should identify meaning at a glance, not make every panel compete. The next visual prototype should apply this direction to only three screens first: the child dashboard, the parent dashboard, and the purchase-request review.

## Light/dark and accessibility guardrails

- Keep the semantic mapping identical in both modes; do not make saving blue in light mode and green in dark mode.
- Change luminance and saturation for dark mode instead of mechanically inverting colors.
- Body text should meet at least a 4.5:1 contrast ratio, while large text should meet at least 3:1, following the W3C's [WCAG 2.2 contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html).
- Never use color as the only cue. Pair spend/save colors with labels, icons, position, or patterns, consistent with the W3C's [use-of-color guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html).
- Editable fields need a consistent border, focus ring, label, and surface in both themes. Calculated values should be rendered as text/result cards, not disabled or differently colored input controls.
- Test accent text on its actual surface. A color that works as a border or icon may not be dark or light enough for small text.

## What not to copy

- **Do not imitate a reference brand's trade dress.** Borrow principles—goal personalization, calm hierarchy, semantic color—not signature layouts, mascots, or exact palettes.
- **Do not gamify risk.** Avoid confetti for simulated trades, pulsing gains, slot-machine motion, “hot” assets, or red/green performance as entertainment. Learning milestones are safer celebration targets.
- **Do not equate “for kids” with maximum saturation.** Too many colored panels erase hierarchy and can make distinct inputs or outputs look functionally related.
- **Do not rely on red versus green.** In addition to accessibility concerns, red/green frames every decision as winning or losing. This app should teach tradeoffs.
- **Do not disguise output as input.** A projection, balance, or interest result should not look like a form field simply because it sits beside one.
- **Do not give the parent and child products unrelated skins.** Shared semantic tokens and components build trust; density, copy, and illustration can adapt by role.
- **Do not imply real brokerage or bank capability.** Avoid “Buy,” “Sell,” “Deposit,” bank-card imagery, live tickers, or other affordances that contradict the simulator's no-real-money boundary.

## Suggested next design exercise

Create one coded or visual child-dashboard prototype in **Growing Garden** with these constraints:

1. One primary evergreen action color.
2. Blue and orange only for the two Pretend Zone scenarios.
3. Gold only for the daily-interest teaching moment.
4. Neutral result cards that cannot be mistaken for inputs.
5. One child-selected goal icon or image.
6. Matching light and dark screenshots at mobile width before propagating the palette through the rest of the app.
